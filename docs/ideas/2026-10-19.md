## The five

### 1. Opening Night

**Pitch:** photograph your menu, sketch your dining room on a napkin, tell us your hours, and be taking real bookings before your soft opening.

**Who hurts today, and how.** A chef signs a lease on a 40-seat space in March and opens in May. In the last two weeks she is fighting the inspector, the dishwasher install and the line cooks' schedule. Her booking setup is a blank SaaS dashboard: create a venue, type 30 menu items with prices, drag 14 tables onto a canvas, set a deposit policy she has no data to choose, embed a widget on a Squarespace site her cousin built. She does the minimum, opens with a broken widget, and her first month of reservations runs through Instagram DMs. In this repo the same story already happened in miniature: the venue-onboarding run found that the self-serve journey dead-ended at the floor plan (`docs/features/venue-onboarding-floor-plan/idea.md`).

**Why now.** Opening an independent restaurant costs a median of about $375,500 ([Katalyst, citing RestaurantOwner.com](https://www.katalystos.com/blog/how-much-does-it-cost-to-open-a-restaurant-in-2026)), and independent locations fell 2.3% in 2025 while chains grew ([NRN](https://www.nrn.com/independent-restaurants/the-independent-restaurant-sector-shrunk-by-2-3-in-2025) **(older)**). The first weeks decide whether that money comes back. Photo-to-menu extraction is now a commodity: QR-menu tools extract items, prices and allergens from a photo ([NordQR changelog, 2026-01](https://www.nordqr.com/changelog/smart-ai-import)). But nobody turns that into a whole operating venue, with tables, policy, and a booking page, in one pass.

**Boldest version.** Opening a restaurant becomes a conversation. The agent drafts the venue from artefacts the owner already has: menu photos, a phone video walk-through of the room (floor plan inferred), the lease's capacity, the neighbourhood. It proposes deposit and cancellation policy from peer data (#4), generates the public booking page and its copy, and pre-seeds a soft-opening invite list with timed friends-and-family slots. For the first 30 days it watches the book and proposes adjustments. Every venue on the platform was "born" by the agent, and the onboarding funnel is the demo.

**First wedge (2-4 weeks).** A "draft my venue" step at the start of the existing wizard: upload a menu photo plus one free-text paragraph, and the agent emits a filled draft through the wizard's existing state (`apps/hospitality/src/components/venue-onboarding/useOnboardingWizard.ts`). That covers menu sections and items into the `Menu`/`MenuSection`/`MenuItem` models (`services/reservations/prisma/schema.prisma`, `services/reservations/src/services/menu.ts`), a template choice and table count from `floor-plan-templates.ts`, and hours via the existing hours step. The owner reviews every field before `LaunchStep.tsx` commits it. The agent's review UI renders through the generative-UI path in `services/agent/src/routes/gen-ui.ts`. Room-photo-to-floor-plan stays out of the wedge.

**What would kill it.** Extraction errors on prices: a wrong price on a live menu is worse than no menu. The review step has to be mandatory and fast, or it's a liability. Second risk: venues this new have no data, so "smart" policy suggestions are guesses until #4 exists.

**Charter fit.** Theme 3 for hospitality, head-on ("polish the onboarding → first-booking journey"). Showcases the agent platform doing real work a customer would pay for.

**Scores.** Ambition 4 · Feasibility 4 · Wow 5

### 2. Headless Venue

**Pitch:** run your restaurant from the AI assistant you already use. Ask ChatGPT "how's Saturday looking?" and it answers from your live book, and it can move the 8pm six-top if you say so.

**Who hurts today, and how.** The owner of three venues is at the fish market at 7am. He wants to know whether Friday at the second venue is oversold, whether the regular who always books the corner booth is coming, and to block the patio for a private event. Today that's three logins on a phone, or a text to a manager who is asleep. He already talks to an AI assistant all day. His restaurant software is the one thing it can't reach.

**Why now.** Restaurant vendors are shipping MCP servers so ChatGPT and Claude can read and change their accounts. Marqii's lets an assistant update holiday hours across locations ([Marqii](https://blog.marqii.com/introducing-marqii-mcp-and-how-to-set-it-up/)). ChatGPT renamed connectors to "apps" on 2025-12-17 and is rolling the platform out ([getdrio](https://www.getdrio.com/blog/what-are-chatgpt-apps)), though write access is still limited by plan ([getdrio, client comparison](https://www.getdrio.com/blog/mcp-client-comparison)). The interface for SMB software is moving into the assistant. The vendor that is native there first wins the owner's attention.

**Boldest version.** The hospitality app becomes optional. Every operator capability is an authorised, audited tool, reachable from any assistant, any channel (voice in the car, a text, a watch), and from the venue's own agents. The web UI stays as the place for dense, visual work (floor plans, the timeline) and as the audit log of what agents did. Distinct from 2026-10-11 #1 (Agent Front Door), which is the guest's agent booking in. This is the owner's agent running the venue, and the two meet in the same tool layer.

**First wedge (2-4 weeks).** The tool layer already exists: `services/agent/src/routes/gen-agent-tools.ts` defines `check_availability`, `lookup_reservation`, `search_guests`, `list_today_reservations`, `create_reservation`, `modify_reservation`, `cancel_reservation`, `seat_walk_in` and `update_table_status` against the reservations service. Wrap the read-only half as a remote MCP server on the existing agent service, scoped by the caller's venue membership (`services/reservations/src/services/member-venues.ts`), and demo it from Claude Desktop answering "how's tonight?" across two venues. Writes come in phase two, behind an explicit confirm step. They touch auth (guardrail 2), so they need human review.

**What would kill it.** Auth and trust: a third-party assistant holding a token that can cancel reservations is a real attack surface, and the charter makes auth-model changes HITL-only. Second: the client platforms' plan gating on writes could make the demo read-only for most viewers.

**Charter fit.** Theme 2 ("how is tonight going?"), delivered outside the app. Strongly showcases the agent platform. No non-goal touched, though the auth guardrail shapes the wedge.

**Scores.** Ambition 4 · Feasibility 5 · Wow 5

### 3. Regulars Club

**Pitch:** your best guests would pay you for first dibs on Friday night. Today they pay American Express and OpenTable for it instead. Sell it yourself.

**Who hurts today, and how.** A 60-seat neighbourhood spot gets hard to book after a good review. Its 200 real regulars, the people who kept it alive through its first winter, now lose prime tables to bots, resellers and the "priority access" windows of card networks and booking apps. The owner gets nothing from those programs. Her regulars feel abandoned. She has no tool to say "these people get the first 48 hours."

**Why now.** Paid priority access is spreading fast. OpenTable Gold Tables covers 500+ restaurants. Access charges up to $3,588 a year, with credits that price tables by demand. Dorsia charges an annual fee. Card issuers pay restaurants to hold seats for cardholders ([Entrepreneur, citing WSJ, 2026-08](https://www.entrepreneur.com/business-news/finding-it-hard-and-harder-to-get-a-restaurant-reservation-some-diners-are-paying-for-priority-access); [TechCrunch on Access](https://techcrunch.com/2024/12/18/access-bets-people-will-pay-thousands-of-dollars-for-guaranteed-restaurant-reservations) **(older)**). The value of a regular is being monetised, but by intermediaries, not by the venue.

**Boldest version.** Every independent venue runs its own membership: tiers, early booking windows, member-only nights, a monthly dining credit that smooths the venue's cash flow, and recognition on arrival. Members of one venue in a group get perks across it. Membership revenue becomes a meaningful, recurring line on a small venue's P&L, and the platform's moat is that it's the venue's list, not the platform's.

**First wedge (2-4 weeks).** A "members-first window" on availability. A guest tagged as a member (`Guest.tags`, `services/reservations/prisma/schema.prisma`) sees slots N days earlier than the public. That's a change to the booking-window calculation in `services/reservations/src/services/slot-rules.ts` (`bookingWindow`, `isSlotInBookingWindow`), with recognition through `services/reservations/src/routes/public-guest-recognition.ts`. Paid membership via Stripe comes later. `Guest.stripeCustomerId` already exists, but subscriptions are a new payment flow and need `stripe-flow-reviewer`. The wedge is free, invite-only membership to prove the mechanics.

**What would kill it.** It only matters for venues hard enough to book that access has value, which is a small slice of the target user. Also optics: "pay to eat at your local" can read as elitist and draw the same backlash the apps get. A venue-run, cash-credit model (the fee comes back as food) is the defence.

**Charter fit.** Theme 1 (guest-facing loop). Mildly challenges the guardrail on new payment surfaces, which should move once deposits are proven stable, because recurring revenue is the clearest "a venue would pay for this" signal the demo could show.

**Scores.** Ambition 4 · Feasibility 3 · Wow 4

### 4. Benchmark Commons

**Pitch:** find out how your Tuesday, your no-show rate and your deposit policy compare with venues like yours, and copy what works.

**Who hurts today, and how.** An owner sees a 6% no-show rate and wonders whether to add a $25 deposit. She has no idea whether 6% is bad. The only benchmarks are published by platforms that sell her the fix: resOS reports 2.33% across 3.77M reservations ([resOS No-Show Index](https://resos.com/restaurant-no-show-rate/)), and Bloom Intelligence reports 3.7% with another 15% cancelled ([Bloom](https://bloomintelligence.com/blog/restaurant-no-shows/)). Those numbers are aggregated over everyone, mix up definitions, and come without the policy that produced them. She guesses, or does nothing.

**Why now.** Benchmarks are being published as marketing. UK no-show data landed on 2026-10-06 ([Restaurant Online](https://www.restaurantonline.co.uk/Article/2026/10/06/no-show-rates-fall-year-on-year-report-finds/)). Meanwhile the big platforms hold the cross-venue data and independents don't. 42% of operators report being unprofitable ([Katalyst](https://www.katalystos.com/blog/how-much-does-it-cost-to-open-a-restaurant-in-2026), secondary), so a policy that recovers even a few covers a week matters.

**Boldest version.** A privacy-preserving data commons owned by participating independents. Each venue contributes aggregates (lead time, party-size mix, no-show and late-cancel rate by policy, turn time by table size, day-of-week curves) under k-anonymity, and gets peer comparisons plus the causal story: "venues like you that added a deposit cut no-shows from 6% to 2% and lost 4% of bookings." Over time it becomes the neutral reference the platforms can't be, and it feeds every other idea here: Opening Night's starting policy, Seat-Yield's prices, Tonight Autopilot's turn times.

**First wedge (2-4 weeks).** A "you vs. peers" panel on the dashboard, computed over all venues in the deployment with a minimum-cohort threshold, built on the per-venue metrics already in `services/reservations/src/services/booking-metrics.ts` (`DailyBookingMetrics`, reservation-status and deposit-transition counts) and the reliability signals in `guest-reliability.ts`. The demo seeds a synthetic cohort and labels it as such. The cross-venue read must respect ADR-026's RLS model: aggregates come from a dedicated, audited path, never a bypass (see the `BYPASSRLS` history in `.claude/rules/gotchas.md` § CI).

**What would kill it.** Cold start: a benchmark of 3 venues is noise, and the demo has few real venues. Trust: owners won't share data unless the anonymisation is visibly solid. RLS: this is the first legitimately cross-tenant read, and getting it wrong is a data leak.

**Charter fit.** Theme 2 (operator daily workflow). Touches the platform theme of observability as a product surface. The cross-tenant read needs an ADR before code.

**Scores.** Ambition 4 · Feasibility 3 · Wow 4

### 5. Safe Plate

_Extends 2026-10-12 seed #9 (allergen chain of custody), which was dropped as "needs a menu model … keep for later". What's new: the repo now has a menu model (`Menu`, `MenuSection`, `MenuItem` in `services/reservations/prisma/schema.prisma`, with `MenusPage.tsx` and `PublicMenuPage.tsx`), so the chain is buildable end to end, and the idea widens from a compliance log to a guest-facing promise._

**Pitch:** tell us about your shellfish allergy once, when you book, and every person who touches your dinner knows, confirms it, and can prove it.

**Who hurts today, and how.** A guest books for four and types "severe sesame allergy" into the notes field. The host reads it at 7pm, maybe. The server is told verbally. The kitchen gets a scribble on the ticket. The dish she orders has tahini in the dressing, which isn't on the menu description. Everyone acted in good faith, the information decayed at every hop, and nobody can say afterwards who knew what.

**Why now.** California's SB 68 took effect on 2026-07-01. It is the first US law requiring restaurants (chains of 20+) to disclose nine major allergens, by menu or QR code ([Kaufman Dolowich, 2026-07-08](https://www.kaufmandolowich.com/news-resources/californias-restaurant-allergen-disclosure-requirements-are-now-in-effect-by-kartikey-pradhan-esq-7-8-2026/)). It doesn't cover the target user, but it resets what guests expect from every restaurant, and laws like it tend to widen. Digital inspection is spreading on the regulator side too: Barcelona's system reports a 34% rise in detected non-compliance in retail settings ([Barcelona](https://ajuntament.barcelona.cat/sanitatisalut/en/noticia/1448875)).

**Boldest version.** A guest's dietary profile is captured once and travels: booking → venue briefing → per-dish allergen flags on the menu → a ticket marker → a kitchen acknowledgement → a server confirmation at the table. Every hop is timestamped. The same rails carry the kitchen's food-safety logbook (temperatures, cleaning) so an inspector or insurer can read one source. Small venues get chain-grade allergen compliance for free, and "we've never lost an allergy" becomes something they can market.

**First wedge (2-4 weeks).** Add the nine SB 68 allergens as an additive field on `MenuItem`, and flag them on the public menu. Then cross-reference a booking's `Guest.dietaryRestrictions` against the active menu in the existing briefing (`services/reservations/src/services/briefing.ts` already returns `dietaryRestrictions` per guest), so the pre-service briefing (`BriefingPage.tsx`) lists "table 12, sesame: 3 dishes on tonight's menu contain it." Finally, record a staff acknowledgement as a `ReservationEvent`. No ticket printing and no POS involvement.

**What would kill it.** Liability. A system that claims to track allergies invites the lawsuit when it fails, and the data is only as good as the menu tagging. It must be framed as an aid with explicit staff confirmation, never as a guarantee. The fully realised version (tickets on the line) also drifts towards POS, a non-goal.

**Charter fit.** Theme 1 (guest-facing loop) and theme 2 (operator workflow). The kitchen-ticket hop challenges the POS non-goal. It should move only to the extent of a printable or screen ticket marker, not real POS integration.

**Scores.** Ambition 3 · Feasibility 4 · Wow 4

## Wildcard: Founders' Table

**Pitch:** your regulars fund your next restaurant by buying their first year of dinners there in advance.

**Who hurts today, and how.** A beloved chef wants a second venue. Banks won't lend to restaurants. Investors want equity and control. The $375K median opening cost ([Katalyst](https://www.katalystos.com/blog/how-much-does-it-cost-to-open-a-restaurant-in-2026)) is out of reach, yet she has 2,000 guests who'd say yes to "help me open the next one."

**Why now.** Savory Fund just raised $201.5M to invest in restaurants ([NRN, 2026-10-13](https://www.nrn.com/restaurant-finance/savory-fund-raises-201-5-million-for-third-restaurant-investment-vehicle)), which shows capital wants restaurant upside. But it's institutional money with institutional terms. Independents keep shrinking ([NRN](https://www.nrn.com/independent-restaurants/the-independent-restaurant-sector-shrunk-by-2-3-in-2025) **(older)**).

**Boldest version (10 years).** A community-finance layer for hospitality. Guests pre-buy dining credit for a venue that doesn't exist yet, with escrow until opening, founding-member perks forever, and a public dashboard of progress. The platform knows (from the Commons and the guest record) which guests are real regulars, so it can underwrite credibility, not just collect money. A neighbourhood can vote a restaurant into existence.

**First wedge.** Honestly, none should ship. A clickable demo flow, a "Founding members" page generated by the gen-UI catalog that reuses the deposit flow's UI (`apps/hospitality/src/pages/PublicBookingPage.tsx`) with no real money, is the most this repo should do.

**What would kill it.** Regulation: pre-sold credit for a business that might never open is close to a security, or at least a gift-card and escrow minefield. 2026-10-11 dropped working-capital lending for the same reason. Then there's fraud, and the reputational blast radius when one founded restaurant fails.

**Charter fit.** Challenges two things: the "no new paid infrastructure" guardrail, and the implicit "we're software, not finance" line. It shouldn't move yet. It's here to show where Regulars Club (#3) leads if it works.

**Scores.** Ambition 5 · Feasibility 1 · Wow 5

## Pick of the week: Opening Night

It is the only idea here that makes the demo's front door the product. A visitor from the marketing site can photograph a menu and watch a venue appear: floor plan, menu, policy and live booking page, generated by the agent platform and reviewed by a human. That is both halves of the leverage test at once: a venue would pay for it (the opening weeks are where independents bleed), and it shows the agent platform doing a real, bounded, reviewable job. It builds directly on the onboarding wizard the repo already shipped, and it's the natural consumer of #4's peer data later.

Runner-up: Headless Venue. It's the cheapest wow in the set (the tools in `gen-agent-tools.ts` already exist), and it could be demoed within a fortnight. It loses only because its write path runs straight into the auth guardrail.
