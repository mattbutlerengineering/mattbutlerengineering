## The five

### 1. Agent Mirror

**Pitch:** Every night, we ask ChatGPT, Google and the other AI assistants to book your restaurant, then tell you every time they got it wrong.

**Who hurts today, and how:** A 60-seat Thai restaurant in Austin is half empty on a Wednesday. A couple asks their assistant for "Thai for two tonight near South Congress". The assistant says the place is fully booked, or closed on Wednesdays (it closed on Wednesdays two years ago), or that it "doesn't take reservations". The couple goes elsewhere. The owner never finds out. Their own booking page is correct, so every dashboard they own says everything is fine. They are being misrepresented in a channel they cannot see.

**Why now:**

- The channel is real and growing fast for incumbents: 190,000 seated diners via ChatGPT for OpenTable in September alone ([Crypto Briefing](https://cryptobriefing.com/opentable-chatgpt-integration-190000-diners/)).
- It has already failed silently in production: a Google change left some venues "appearing to have no available reservations at all", "without notification to restaurant owners" ([PPC Land](https://ppc.land/googles-ai-mode-now-books-uk-restaurants-meet-the-8-partner-platforms/)).
- Nobody owns the measurement. Booking platforms are inside the agent deals, so they are not neutral auditors of them.

**Boldest version:** The "Search Console" of the agent era, for venues. A fleet of synthetic diners, with different party sizes, dates, languages, dietary needs and locations, asks every major assistant about every venue on the platform, every day. Each answer is diffed against ground truth: availability, hours, closures, menu, fees and policies. The owner sees an "agent accuracy" score per assistant, a list of wrong claims with the evidence, and one-click fixes for the ones the venue controls (publish a correction to the facts feed, update the Google profile, file a correction with the provider). Across the network, the aggregate becomes the industry's only independent benchmark of how well AI agents represent restaurants, and a dataset that agent companies want to buy back to fix their products.

**First wedge (2–4 weeks):** A "what the AIs say about you" panel.

- A probe job in `services/agent` asks an LLM with web search a fixed set of guest questions about a demo venue ("table for 4 Saturday 7pm?", "open Monday?", "is there a deposit?").
- Ground truth comes from the engine the booking widget already uses: `services/reservations/src/services/availability-snapshot.ts`, `routes/public-availability.ts`, `routes/public-venues.ts`, and closures.
- Each answer is scored match, mismatch or unknown, and stored with a timestamp. That stored record is seed #17 (misquote insurance) in miniature.
- A new `gen-agent-*-tools.ts` tool renders the latest probe as a Rialto card ("2 of 7 answers wrong: claims closed Mondays").
- Probe only through APIs whose terms allow it. No scraping of consumer assistants.

**What would kill it:** Access. The assistants that matter most may refuse or rate-limit synthetic probing, or forbid it in their terms, and answers differ by account, location and personalisation, so one probe is not "what guests see". It also has a fix gap: if a venue can't correct the error, a dashboard of wrong answers is just anxiety.

**Charter fit:** Theme (1), close the guest-facing loop: the loop now runs through agents. It challenges guardrail 1, "no new paid infrastructure", because probing uses metered LLM calls. It should bend for this: it is an operating cost per probe, not new infrastructure, and the agent service already pays for LLM calls.

**Scores:** ambition 4 · feasibility 4 · portfolio wow 5

### 2. Channel Ledger

**Pitch:** See which booking channels send you guests who come back, and which ones send you one-time bargain hunters who no-show, then decide how much of Saturday each channel gets.

**Who hurts today, and how:** A two-venue group in Chicago takes bookings from its own site, Google, OpenTable, an AI assistant integration, a hotel concierge and the phone. Every channel reports "covers". None reports what those covers were worth over a year. The owner suspects the OpenTable diners are mostly one-and-done and the website bookers are the regulars, but can't prove it. So they keep paying per-cover fees for all of it, and give every channel equal access to 8pm Saturday.

**Why now:**

- New channels are arriving fast, and they don't look alike: OpenTable says its AI/LLM-channel diners spend about 20% more ([Crypto Briefing](https://cryptobriefing.com/opentable-chatgpt-integration-190000-diners/), unattributed figure). The owner wants that number for their own venue, not the platform's.
- Hotel-style channel management has reached restaurants: SevenRooms Channel Connect syncs inventory across platforms, but sells sync, not guest value ([Restaurant Technology News](https://restauranttechnologynews.com/2026/06/sevenrooms-launches-channel-connect-to-bring-hotel-style-distribution-management-to-restaurants/)).
- Traffic is split almost evenly between operators gaining and losing guests ([NRA](https://www.restaurant.org/research-and-media/research/restaurant-economic-insights/economic-indicators/same-store-sales-and-customer-traffic)). When traffic is scarce, knowing which channel brings guests worth keeping matters more.

**Boldest version:** A hotel-grade revenue desk for restaurants. Every reservation carries its channel. Every guest carries a cohort: first channel, then repeat rate, no-show rate, spend and lifetime value. The owner gets a ledger: channel cost (fees, commissions, per-cover charges) against channel value (12-month guest worth). Then allocation: "ChatGPT gets 4 tables at 8pm Saturdays; OpenTable gets 2, and only for first-timers", enforced across channels, adjusted automatically as the ledger learns. Over five years, venues stop asking "how many covers did this channel bring?" and start asking "what did this channel cost per returning guest?", which is the question that makes them independent of any one platform.

**First wedge (2–4 weeks):**

- Extend the origin capture already on `Reservation.originHost` (`services/reservations/prisma/schema.prisma`, set in `routes/public-reservations.ts`) to a `channel` value: referrer, a `?via=` parameter on the booking widget link, and known agent user agents. This is an additive migration.
- A cohort report per channel: second-visit rate (`Guest.visitCount`), no-show rate (`Guest.noShowCount`, `guest-reliability.ts`), and deposit forfeits (`deposit-state-machine.ts` terminal states). It follows the counts-only shape of `services/booking-metrics.ts`, so no PII leaks into the report.
- An agent tool answers "which channel brings my regulars?" as a Rialto chart card.
- Allocation stays out of the wedge.

**What would kill it:** Attribution. Third-party channels book inside their own systems, so the venue sees only a sync, and a guest who first found you on Google and then booked on your site looks like a direct booker. If most volume is off-platform, the ledger is thin. Allocation also needs two-way inventory control the big platforms don't grant to rivals.

**Charter fit:** Theme (2), operator daily workflow: an owner-level view. No non-goal challenged. Third-party channel ingestion would brush the spirit of the POS-integration non-goal, but this is booking data, not POS.

**Scores:** ambition 3 · feasibility 4 · portfolio wow 3

### 3. Occasion Desk

**Pitch:** When a guest books for a birthday, sell them the birthday: cake, card, champagne, flowers, a photo. It's arranged in two taps, paid up front, and on the table when they sit down.

**Who hurts today, and how:** A guest books a Denver steakhouse for their partner's 40th, ticks "birthday", and then calls twice to ask whether they can bring a cake, whether there's a corkage fee, and whether someone can put a candle in it. The host writes "BDAY - cake?" in the notes. On the night the cake is in the walk-in, the candle is missing, and the server sings to the wrong table. The venue made $0 from the most emotionally valuable booking of the week and nearly ruined it.

**Why now:**

- Drink margin is under pressure. GLP-1 users cut alcohol and dessert first, and visit about half as often ([William Blair](https://www.williamblair.com/Insights/GLP1s-and-Restaurants-A-Behavioral-Shift-Not-a-Demand-Collapse)). Venues need check growth that isn't a second bottle.
- Traffic is flat to down for nearly half of operators ([NRA](https://www.restaurant.org/research-and-media/research/restaurant-economic-insights/economic-indicators/same-store-sales-and-customer-traffic)). That makes revenue per occasion more valuable than one more cover.
- Prepayment changes behaviour: protected bookings no-show at about a third of the rate of unprotected ones ([resOS](https://resos.com/restaurant-no-show-rate/), observational). A prepaid cake is a protected booking the guest wanted to pay for.

**Boldest version:** Every booked occasion is produced. The venue sets up a catalogue once: its own add-ons (cake, a wine pairing, a private corner, a printed menu with the couple's names) plus a marketplace of local vendors (florist, photographer, musician), with the venue taking a margin. An agent turns "it's our 10th anniversary, she loves peonies" into a priced proposal on the manage-booking page. It coordinates the vendors, puts a run-sheet on the kitchen and floor ("Table 12, 8:40pm: dessert with candle, then photographer"), and sends the guest a keepsake afterwards. The occasion becomes a line of business with its own margin, and the venue becomes the place in town where you book life's moments.

**First wedge (2–4 weeks):**

- Occasion add-ons as priced items, modelled on `MenuItem` (`priceCents`) in `services/reservations/prisma/schema.prisma`.
- Offered when `Reservation.occasion` is `birthday` or `anniversary`, on `apps/hospitality/src/pages/ManageReservationPage.tsx` and in the booking confirmation.
- Paid through the existing Stripe path (`routes/public-deposits.ts`, `services/deposit.ts`), as a prepayment line, not a deposit state.
- The add-ons appear on the night's run-sheet in `services/briefing.ts` / `apps/hospitality/src/pages/BriefingPage.tsx`, next to remembered-on-arrival's occasion flag.
- No third-party vendors in the wedge.

**What would kill it:** Execution liability. Once the venue charges for the cake, a forgotten candle is a refund and a one-star review, not a shrug. Third-party vendors bring payments, tax and marketplace-liability questions a solo engineer shouldn't take on. Some guests will also find upsells on a birthday booking tacky. It must stay an offer, never a gate.

**Charter fit:** Theme (1), close the guest-facing loop, and theme (2), via the run-sheet. It extends `docs/features/remembered-on-arrival` from acknowledgement to fulfilment. The vendor marketplace in the boldest version challenges the charter's scope (it is not a marketplace product). That should only move once the venue-only add-ons prove guests pay.

**Scores:** ambition 3 · feasibility 5 · portfolio wow 4

### 4. No Host Stand

**Pitch:** Run your door without a host: guests check in, join the wait or get seated at a tablet, and your staff only hear about it when a table needs them.

**Who hurts today, and how:** A 32-seat ramen counter in Portland runs with a chef, two cooks and one server. It can't afford a host, and since enforcement thinned its kitchen it can't spare anyone for the door. On Friday the server is taking orders while six parties pile up at the entrance. Two walk-ins leave after nobody greets them. A reservation is seated at a table promised to someone else. The owner's options are to pay $20 an hour for a host or to keep losing the door.

**Why now:**

- Labor costs keep rising: 77% of operators saw them rise in H1 2026 (Restaurant365, cited 2026-11-09). Labor tools are a proven product line: Toast's Scheduling Pro customers averaged 41% fewer overtime hours per employee ([Toast Q3 call, Webull transcript](https://www.webull.com/news/15862269284293632)).
- Kitchens are thinner after immigration enforcement (cited 2026-11-09). The front of house is the first place a small venue cuts.
- Guests already accept self-service at the counter. AI phone hosts like Hostie now take whole orders without staff ([Fast Casual](https://www.fastcasual.com/news/hostie-adds-takeout-ordering-to-ai-powered-restaurant-concierge/)). The door is the remaining manual step.

**Boldest version:** The door becomes software. A tablet (later, the door camera) greets every party by name if they booked. It quotes an honest wait for walk-ins, picks the table using the same engine that books online, and shows the guest a lit path on the floor plan to their seat. It flips the table's state, so the kitchen sees a new cover and the server gets a "table 7 seated, 2 covers, nut allergy" buzz. When it can't decide (a VIP, a complaint, a party of 9), it calls a human, and only then. Small venues run with one fewer role. Large ones redeploy the host to hospitality instead of logistics.

**First wedge (2–4 weeks):** A kiosk mode for the hospitality PWA.

- Reservation lookup at the door (`services/guest-recognition.ts` already does a safe-field lookup by email; name or phone lookup is new), or walk-in entry into `routes/public-waitlist.ts`.
- Table choice from `services/bookable-tables.ts` and `services/waitlist-estimate.ts`.
- Seating goes through `services/table-state-machine.ts`, and staff tablets get an SSE event through `services/sse-connection-manager.ts`.
- `apps/hospitality/src/pages/WaitlistPage.tsx` is the starting UI.
- The demo: a guest walks up, taps, and the floor plan lights a table.

**What would kill it:** Hospitality culture. A tablet greeting is the opposite of what many full-service guests pay for, so this may only ever fit counters, fast casual and late-night venues. The engine also has to be trusted to seat without a human override, and one bad seating (a pram at a high-top) does real damage at the door.

**Charter fit:** Theme (2), operator daily workflow. It brushes the "payroll/staffing" non-goal: it changes how many staff are needed but touches no payroll or scheduling. It stays inside "no native mobile apps" because it's PWA kiosk mode.

**Scores:** ambition 4 · feasibility 4 · portfolio wow 4

### 5. The Book as Balance Sheet

**Pitch:** Your reservations book is money you haven't been paid yet, so use it to get a loan on fair terms, or an automatic payout when a storm wipes out a booked Saturday.

_Evolves 2026-10-12 seed #11 (forward-book cash forecast) and 2026-10-19 seed #13 (venue health score sold to lenders). What's new is the external counterparty: the book is underwritten by a lender or insurer, not just shown to the owner._

**Who hurts today, and how:** A Charleston seafood restaurant has 1,400 covers booked over the next 60 days, including two prepaid rehearsal dinners, and $9,000 in held deposits. A walk-in cooler dies in August. The bank wants two years of tax returns and three weeks. The POS lender's offer is priced off past card sales from a slow quarter and repaid as a cut of every swipe. Neither can see the booked future that makes this a safe loan. In September a hurricane warning closes the venue for two booked nights. The business-interruption claim takes months and needs paperwork the owner doesn't have.

**Why now:**

- Embedded lending is already a big business and a risk line for the incumbent: analysts flag Toast's credit-loss expense and loan purchases ([Finsee](https://finsee.ai/earnings/tost/2026/q3/en/)). Toast underwrites on past payments. Nobody underwrites on booked demand.
- Card-fee relief isn't coming: the settlement trims about 0.1% ([Beancount](https://beancount.io/blog/2026/07/09/visa-mastercard-interchange-settlement-surcharging-guide), vendor guide). Cheaper capital is a margin lever that remains.
- Deposits now reliably turn bookings into realised covers: protected bookings no-show at 0.80% against 2.47% ([resOS](https://resos.com/restaurant-no-show-rate/)). That realisation rate is exactly what an underwriter needs.

**Boldest version:** The forward book becomes a financial instrument. The platform publishes a signed, continuously updated statement of booked covers, held deposits, historical realisation rate by day and channel, and a confidence band. Lenders compete to offer credit lines against it, repaid from future deposits rather than every card swipe. Insurers write parametric cover: "if a weather closure cancels booked covers, pay $X per lost cover within 48 hours", priced from the venue's own history and triggered by the closure the operator records in the platform. In five years, a small venue's reservations system is also its credit file, and a full book is worth something on the day it is booked, not only on the night.

**First wedge (2–4 weeks):** A read-only "forward book statement" page.

- Next 30, 60 and 90 days of booked covers.
- Held deposits as a liability line, from `Deposit` rows in `held` state (`services/deposit-state-machine.ts`).
- Realisation rate from the last 12 months' completed-versus-no-show outcomes (`guest-reliability.ts` inputs, `services/booking-metrics.ts`).
- A "what if we close this date?" panel built on `services/closure-impact.ts`, which already computes which future bookings a closure would leave without service. Priced at average cover value, it becomes the lost-revenue figure a parametric policy would pay.
- An agent tool renders it as a statement card an owner could screenshot for their bank.
- No lender integration in the wedge.

**What would kill it:** Regulation and trust. Lending and insurance are licensed activities. Any real version needs a regulated partner, and a lender won't trust a statement the borrower's own software produced unless it is tamper-evident and audited. Bookings are also soft collateral: a bad review or a recession can drain a book in a week, which is exactly when the loan is due.

**Charter fit:** It fits no current theme. It challenges the charter's scope: the hospitality demo is defined as reservations and operations, not finance. It should move because the statement wedge is pure read-only reporting over data the repo already holds, and "your book is worth money" is the most compelling thing a venue owner could hear from their reservations software. The lender/insurer layer stays out until a partner exists.

**Scores:** ambition 5 · feasibility 3 · portfolio wow 4

## Wildcard: Covers Currency

_Evolves Founders' Table (2026-10-19 wildcard), where regulars pre-buy future dinners at one venue. What's new: the pre-bought dinners become interoperable, a city-wide currency that independents issue together._

**Pitch:** A dining currency that only independents can issue, backed by seats they will actually serve, and spendable at any of them without a card fee.

**Who hurts today, and how:** Every independent in a neighbourhood pays card fees on every check, sells gift cards that only work at one place (and often go unspent), and watches loyalty apps collect guest data for someone else's benefit.

**Why now:** Blackbird just showed diners will carry a restaurant-specific currency in a real debit card, with spend data feeding an AI recommender ([NRN](https://www.nrn.com/restaurant-technology/tech-tracker-blackbird-launches-a-debit-card)). The card-fee settlement leaves interchange almost untouched ([Beancount](https://beancount.io/blog/2026/07/09/visa-mastercard-interchange-settlement-surcharging-guide)).

**Boldest version (10-year horizon):** Each independent can mint "covers" against its own forward book: a credit worth one dinner, redeemable on a future date it chooses to make available. Covers settle between venues at near-zero cost on a shared ledger the venues govern. A regular's balance is spendable at the taco place, the wine bar and the bakery. Pre-sales fund openings and slow months. The network effect stays local: a neighbourhood's restaurants become one economy instead of 40 tenants each renting a card terminal.

**First wedge:** A single-group prototype: a stored-value "house credit" spendable across the venues of one `VenueGroup` (`services/reservations/prisma/schema.prisma`). Issued through the existing Stripe path, it pays down a deposit on a sister venue's booking.

**What would kill it:** Money-transmission and stored-value regulation (state by state), unredeemed-balance (escheatment) law, and the hard truth that a currency is only as good as its weakest issuer. One closure strands everyone's balance.

**Charter fit:** It challenges "no new paid infrastructure" and the charter's scope entirely. That is on purpose: it is a wildcard.

**Scores:** ambition 5 · feasibility 1 · portfolio wow 4

## Pick of the week: Agent Mirror

Agent Mirror is the bet, for three reasons.

1. **The pain is new, real and invisible.** Agent channels went from zero to six figures of monthly diners at one platform in a single month. When the channel is wrong, it fails silently, exactly as Google's change did. No owner can see it today, so the first demo ("here's what three AIs told a guest about you last night, and two were wrong") lands in five seconds with any venue owner.
2. **It showcases the agent platform honestly.** Agents audit agents, scored against a ground truth the repo already computes (`availability-snapshot.ts`, the closure engine). The result renders as Rialto cards through the existing `gen-agent-*` tool pattern. It's the charter's "self-improving" story turned outward at the market.
3. **It compounds with earlier picks.** It is the measurement half of Agent Front Door (2026-10-11 pick) and Wait Truth (2026-10-26 #4): those publish the truth, and Mirror proves whether agents use it.

The risk is assistant access and terms. The wedge must use only API surfaces whose terms allow programmatic queries, and say so on the page.

Runner-up: Occasion Desk. It is the most feasible, and it turns a feature the repo just shipped (remembered-on-arrival) into revenue a venue can count.
