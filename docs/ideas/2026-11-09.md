## The five

### 1. Daypart Forge

**Pitch:** Your room is a restaurant for five hours a day; run it as three restaurants, a morning café, a lunch counter and a dinner room, each with its own name, menu and booking page, from one account.

**Who hurts today, and how:** A 50-seat neighbourhood bistro in Philadelphia pays rent 24 hours a day and fills the room from 5:30 to 10. The owner wants a weekday breakfast-and-coffee concept to pay the rent without touching the dinner brand. Today that means a second Instagram account, a second booking page from a different vendor, a printed menu, and a host who rearranges the tables by hand at 3pm. Guests who book "the bistro" for breakfast get the wrong expectations, and the dinner room's reviews take the hit. So the owner never does it, and the room sits dark for 14 hours.

**Why now:**

- The multi-concept model works at scale. Wonder runs 20 to 30 concepts out of each kitchen across about 170 locations, and filed for an IPO on 2026-11-05 ([MarketScale](https://www.marketscale.com/industries/food-beverage/wonder-confidential-ipo-filing-grubhub-owner), [QSR](https://www.qsrmagazine.com/growth/fast-casual/wonder-takes-first-step-toward-ipo/)). Independents have no software to do the same with one room.
- Fixed costs are not falling. 77% of operators saw labor costs rise in H1 ([Restaurant365](https://www.restaurant365.com/guides/2026-state-of-the-restaurant-industry-mid-year-report/)), and beef is at record prices ([Chowhound](https://www.chowhound.com/2177588/why-beef-so-expensive-2026/)). Revenue per square foot per day is the lever left.
- Independents are closing. New York is at a 36-year low ([Business Today](https://www.businesstoday.in/world/story/36-year-low-new-york-restaurant-count-plunges-by-2000-in-q1-2026-amid-soaring-costs-562283-2026-10-18)).

**Boldest version:** A "concept" becomes the unit of a restaurant, not the venue. One room hosts many brands across the day and week: breakfast café, lunch counter, Tuesday supper club, Sunday brunch. Each has its own public identity, menu, floor plan, booking rules and guest list, and they share one kitchen, one staff roster and one ledger. An agent drafts a new concept from one sentence ("a $14 lunch bowl counter, weekdays 11 to 2"): name, menu from the existing pantry, a counter-seating floor plan, schedule, and booking page. It forecasts the incremental contribution from the venue's own history. The owner's dashboard shows revenue per seat-hour across all concepts, and concepts that don't pay are sunset with one click, their guests offered the sibling concept. Over five years the product stops being "reservations for a restaurant" and becomes "an operating system for a room".

**First wedge (2–4 weeks):** Concepts as a thin layer over what already exists.

- A `VenueService` (`services/reservations/prisma/schema.prisma`) gets an optional public identity: a display name, a menu link (`Menu` already has a per-venue `slug`), an optional floor plan, and an accent colour. This is an additive migration.
- `PublicBookingPage.tsx` and `PublicMenusPage.tsx` render that identity when the guest books a slot in that service, so "Bistro Céleste" at 7pm reads as "Céleste Café" at 8am.
- The services filter on the availability calendar (#8409) becomes a concept switcher.
- The demo: an agent tool in `services/agent/src/routes/gen-agent-menu-tools.ts`-style ("draft a lunch concept") proposes a menu, a `ScheduleService` and a public page, previewed through `packages/rialto-catalog/src/catalog.ts`, for the owner to accept.

**What would kill it:** Operations, not software. A second concept needs a second prep flow, and staff fatigue is real. If owners won't staff the extra daypart, the software is irrelevant. Brand confusion on Google and agent surfaces (one address, three names) could also hurt more than it helps.

**Charter fit:** Theme (2), the operator's daily workflow, and theme (3), onboarding (a new concept is a second onboarding). Kitchen and staff operations stay out of scope: the wedge touches booking and identity only, so it does not challenge the payroll/staffing non-goal.

**Scores:** ambition 5, feasibility 4, wow 5.

### 2. Mystery Seat

**Pitch:** Sell your empty 5:45 seats to diners who choose a price and a vibe, not a name, so you fill the room without ever advertising a discount.

**Who hurts today, and how:** A respected 80-seat Italian place has 30 empty seats every Tuesday before 7. Posting "20% off Tuesdays" on its own page cheapens the brand and trains regulars to wait for the deal. Discount apps take a cut and attract one-time bargain hunters who never return. So the owner leaves the seats empty, eats the labor already scheduled, and hopes.

**Why now:**

- Diners are trading down. 36% spent less at restaurants in Q2 than the prior quarter ([NRA Q2 survey summary](https://www.pulse.bot/food-and-beverage/news/national-restaurant-association-quarterly-consumer-insights-survey-q2-2026-48e2b3fb-3c90-45ef-a8cc-7d349d2584ef/)).
- Value channels are being fought over by giants. Amazon moved its Prime restaurant perk to Uber Eats on 2026-11-05 ([Finimize](https://finimize.com/content/wonder-group-quietly-preps-an-ipo-as-it-eyes-drones)).
- Labor is the fixed cost of an empty seat: 77% of operators saw labor costs rise ([Restaurant365](https://www.restaurant365.com/guides/2026-state-of-the-restaurant-industry-mid-year-report/)).
- Hotels and air travel proved the opaque model protects list price (Hotwire, Priceline's "Express Deals"). That point is first-principles; I did not search for current data.

**Boldest version:** A city-wide opaque channel for tonight's slack. A guest (or their agent) says "two of us, 6pm, within 15 minutes, $50 a head, no shellfish, somewhere quiet". The network matches across venues that have opted in, takes payment, and only then reveals the venue. Venues set floor prices per service and day, and never appear in a public list of deals. The match is weighted by guest reliability, so no-show risk is priced in, and a guest's first Mystery Seat visit flows straight into the venue's own guest record and win-back. Over time it is the best acquisition channel an independent has: guests who would never have picked the place arrive, and a measurable share come back at full price.

**First wedge (2–4 weeks):** A single-venue version, which is honest about network density.

- The operator marks off-peak slots as "mystery-eligible" with a floor price per head. This is an additive field on the service `rules` JSON in `ScheduleService`.
- A public `/mystery` page lists blind offers ("A neighbourhood Italian, 6:00, $45pp") across the demo's venues, reusing `routes/public-availability.ts`.
- Booking runs through the existing hold and deposit path (`services/reservations/src/services/hold.ts`, `confirm-hold.ts`, `routes/public-deposits.ts`, using the `per_person` deposit in `packages/cancellation-policy/src/deposit-quote.ts`), and the venue is revealed on `ManageReservationPage.tsx` after payment.
- `guest-reliability.ts` gates eligibility, so a guest with a no-show history can't take a blind seat.
- The follow-through: tag the guest `source: mystery` and measure how many book again at full price. That is the whole thesis in one number.

**What would kill it:** Network density. Opaque inventory needs many venues in one neighbourhood, and a demo with seeded venues can't prove that a real market clears. Fee-disclosure laws are a second risk: "first advertised price" rules ([Mass.gov](https://www.mass.gov/doc/junk-fee-regulations-for-restaurants/download)) mean the blind price must be all-in, with service charge included.

**Charter fit:** Theme (1), closing the guest-facing loop (booking, deposit, confirmation). It challenges no non-goal. A real multi-venue network would eventually need new infrastructure, and guardrail 1 (no new paid infrastructure) would apply then, not to the wedge.

**Scores:** ambition 4, feasibility 4, wow 4.

### 3. Babel Floor

**Pitch:** Every guest books, reads the menu and gets messages in their own language, and every staffer works the host stand and the kitchen in theirs, with the software translating everything in between.

**Who hurts today, and how:** A Houston taquería-turned-bistro has a Spanish-speaking kitchen, an English-speaking front of house, and growing numbers of Vietnamese- and Portuguese-speaking guests. An allergy note typed in English at booking ("no tree nuts, severe") reaches the line as a scribble the prep cook can't read. When two kitchen staff stop coming in, the owner hires whoever can start Monday, and the new dishwasher can't read the station guide. Guests who don't read English call instead of booking online, and the phone isn't answered during service.

**Why now:**

- About one in five restaurant workers is an immigrant, concentrated in kitchen roles, and enforcement is thinning those kitchens: 36% of Texas operators reported losing staff to it ([Latin Times](https://www.latintimes.com/more-third-texas-restaurants-say-they-lost-workers-due-immigration-enforcement-report-finds-598756), [Documented](https://documentedny.com/2025/10/29/trump-immigration-immigrant-workers-restaurant-impact/)). Whoever replaces them must be productive on day one, in any language.
- 77% of operators saw labor costs rise ([Restaurant365](https://www.restaurant365.com/guides/2026-state-of-the-restaurant-industry-mid-year-report/)), so a new hire who can't work the system on day one is expensive.
- Machine translation of short operational text is now good enough to use at the pass. That is first-principles reasoning, not a cited claim.
- The repo already did the hard plumbing. Locale catalogs (`en-US`, `en-GB`, `es-MX`, `fr-CA`) and guest-language closure notices (#8406) shipped in October.

**Boldest version:** Language stops being a constraint on who can work or eat anywhere. Each guest and each staffer has a language. Every artifact, from booking page, confirmations, menu, host-stand view and kitchen ticket to allergy alert and pre-shift briefing, renders in the reader's language from one canonical source. Free text a guest typed ("my mother uses a wheelchair, near the door please") is translated and structured on the way in. Safety-critical notes (allergens) render as pictograms with the original text attached, never translation alone. The pre-shift briefing is read aloud to each staffer in their language. At the five-year mark, front of house wears earpieces: the guest speaks Portuguese and the server hears English. The venue can hire for skill and attitude, not for English.

**First wedge (2–4 weeks):** Per-reader language on the two surfaces that matter most.

- Staff get a language preference, alongside the guest one that already drives `closure-notice-language`. This is additive.
- `BriefingPage.tsx` and the briefing agent tool (`services/agent/src/routes/gen-agent-briefing-tools.ts`) render tonight's briefing in the staffer's language, including guest notes translated at render time with the original shown beneath.
- Guest free-text notes (allergies, accessibility) are machine-translated into the venue's kitchen language, and known allergens are mapped to icons. This builds on the `Guest.dietaryRestrictions` field.
- The booking widget and menus gain the existing `es-MX` and `fr-CA` catalogs end to end (`apps/hospitality/src/i18n/catalogs/`). The demo: book in French, and watch the host see English and the kitchen see Spanish.

**What would kill it:** Trust in machine translation of safety-critical text. One mistranslated allergy note that harms a guest ends the product. Mitigations (pictograms, original text always shown, "confirm with guest" prompts) slow it down. A second risk: staff-facing language features edge toward HR tooling, the payroll/staffing non-goal.

**Charter fit:** Theme (1), the guest loop (multilingual booking and confirmations), and theme (2), the operator's daily workflow (briefings). It touches the staff side of the staffing non-goal only through how staff read the screens. It doesn't schedule, pay or hire, so it doesn't challenge the non-goal.

**Scores:** ambition 4, feasibility 4, wow 4.

### 4. Standing Table

**Pitch:** Let your best guests own "Thursday at 7, table for two, every week", so they never have to fight for it and you start every week with a third of the book already sold.

**Who hurts today, and how:** A couple has eaten at the same Brooklyn wine bar every Thursday for three years. Every week one of them opens the app, finds 7pm gone or held by a bot, books 8:45, and grumbles. The owner would happily reserve their table forever, but the system has no concept of "always", so the host keeps a paper list and sometimes double-books it. Meanwhile the operator has no idea what next month looks like, because every booking is a one-off.

**Why now:**

- Sniping is getting people banned. Resy has banned diners whose agents hammered for tables ([Restaurant Business](https://www.restaurantbusinessonline.com/technology/ai-agents-can-help-diners-book-table-it-can-also-get-them-banned)). A standing table gives a guest's agent something sanctioned to manage instead of something to snipe.
- Consumer agents that manage recurring tasks for a subscription now exist: Meta's Muse at $20/month ([gagadget](https://gagadget.com/en/725170-metas-muse-ai-agent-can-shop-book-and-email-on-your-behalf-for-20-a-month/)).
- Venues need predictable revenue. Diners are pulling back ([NRA Q2 summary](https://www.pulse.bot/food-and-beverage/news/national-restaurant-association-quarterly-consumer-insights-survey-q2-2026-48e2b3fb-3c90-45ef-a8cc-7d349d2584ef/)), and closures are at historic highs in New York ([Business Today](https://www.businesstoday.in/world/story/36-year-low-new-york-restaurant-count-plunges-by-2000-in-q1-2026-amid-soaring-costs-562283-2026-10-18)).

**Boldest version:** Recurring demand becomes the backbone of the book. A standing table is a contract: slot, cadence, party size, house rules (skip with 48 hours' notice; three unannounced skips and it lapses). A forward-book view shows each venue its contracted base load per service for the next 90 days. Guests' agents manage their standing tables, skipping, moving or inviting friends, through a sanctioned API rather than scraping. When a standing guest skips, the slot is released to the waitlist automatically. Standing tables can be gifted, handed to a friend for a month, or bequeathed when a couple moves away. A venue with 30% contracted base load can show it to a landlord or lender (an evolution I'd revisit once the base exists). It also makes Second Act (2026-11-02 #2) concrete: the asset that follows the chef is a list of standing tables.

**First wedge (2–4 weeks):** Recurring reservations with skip and lapse.

- A `StandingReservation` row (an additive migration): guest, venue, service, weekday, time, party size, cadence and start date.
- A nightly job materialises each upcoming instance as a normal `Reservation` N days ahead. It reuses the job runner in `services/reservations/src/services/job-worker.ts` and the booking path in `book-slot.ts`, so conflicts, holds and closures all behave as they do today. A closure already notifies the guest through the existing closure pipeline.
- The guest manages it from `ManageReservationPage.tsx` (skip this week, pause, cancel) using the existing manage-token scope (`routes/manage-token-scope.ts`).
- `lapse-detector.ts` already knows a guest's average visit frequency. It drives an operator prompt: "Maya and Sam have come 11 Thursdays in a row. Offer them a standing table?"
- `contact-policy.ts` governs every message the feature sends.

**What would kill it:** Flexibility. If standing tables squeeze out walk-ins and first-timers on peak nights, the room ossifies and new demand goes elsewhere. Operators will need a cap per service, and setting that cap well is a policy problem (Policy Wind Tunnel, 2026-11-02 #1, is the natural tool). Guests may also simply prefer spontaneity, so demand for standing slots may be thin outside regulars.

**Charter fit:** Theme (1), closing the guest-facing loop (a booking that manages itself), and theme (2), the operator's daily workflow (forward-book base load). It challenges no non-goal.

**Scores:** ambition 4, feasibility 5, wow 4.

### 5. Concept Lab

**Pitch:** Before you put a new dish or a tasting night on the menu, pre-sell it to your own guests. If 40 people put down $10, you cook it; if not, you've lost nothing.

**Who hurts today, and how:** A chef-owner wants to run a $95 Basque tasting menu on Sundays. It means two weeks of R&D, a new supplier, and staff training. She launches it on gut feel, sells 11 covers the first Sunday, and kills it after a month, $4,000 down. With beef at record prices, she also wants to swap the steak frites for a cheaper braise, but has no way to know whether regulars will follow her, so the steak stays and the margin bleeds.

**Why now:**

- Ingredient costs are volatile. Record beef prices hit chains and independents alike, and are expected to stay high through 2027 ([Chowhound](https://www.chowhound.com/2177588/why-beef-so-expensive-2026/), [RFD-TV](https://www.rfdtv.com/inflation-and-beef-costs-pressure-restaurant-store-counts?p=37)). Menus must change faster than gut feel allows.
- Diners are pulling back ([NRA Q2 summary](https://www.pulse.bot/food-and-beverage/news/national-restaurant-association-quarterly-consumer-insights-survey-q2-2026-48e2b3fb-3c90-45ef-a8cc-7d349d2584ef/)), so a failed launch costs more than it used to.
- Wonder runs dozens of concepts per kitchen and can test them on volume ([QSR](https://www.qsrmagazine.com/growth/fast-casual/wonder-takes-first-step-toward-ipo/)). An independent can only test with its own guests.

**Boldest version:** Every menu change is an experiment with real money behind it. A new dish, a tasting night, a brunch, a price change or a swap is posted to the venue's own guests (lapsed regulars, standing tables, recent visitors) as a pre-sale with a threshold and a deadline. Below threshold, nobody is charged. Above it, the kitchen commits, with a known cover count to buy and prep against. The results accumulate into a per-venue demand model ("your guests follow you on fish, not on price cuts"), and an agent proposes the next experiment. Over years, an independent gets the R&D discipline of a chain with none of the chain's sameness.

**First wedge (2–4 weeks):** A pre-sale with a threshold, built on deposits.

- The operator creates an "experiment" in `MenusPage.tsx`: a draft `MenuItem` (the existing `status` enum gains `EXPERIMENT` through an additive migration) or a dated special service, plus a threshold and a deadline.
- Guests commit on a public page next to `PublicMenusPage.tsx`, using card authorisation without capture. The money flow reuses `services/reservations/src/services/deposit-state-machine.ts` and `stripe.ts`, so `held` → `applied` on success and released on a miss. The `stripe-flow-reviewer` agent exists for exactly this kind of change.
- Invitations go to guests chosen by `lapse-detector.ts` and visit history, gated by `contact-policy.ts`.
- On threshold, an agent drafts the `DateRule` for the special night, and the existing availability engine sells the remaining seats.

**What would kill it:** Most independents don't change menus deliberately enough to want experiments, and guests may find "back my dish" odd unless the venue has a following. Authorisation holds also expire (typically within 7 days for cards), which caps the experiment window unless it switches to deposits.

**Charter fit:** Theme (2), the operator's daily workflow, and theme (1), the guest loop (a new reason to come back). It challenges no non-goal. It deliberately stays clear of food-cost data, which would brush the POS integration non-goal.

**Scores:** ambition 3, feasibility 4, wow 4.

## Wildcard: The Roaming Restaurant

**Pitch:** You book the chef, not the address; tonight's room is chosen the day before from whichever rooms in the city are idle.

**Who hurts today, and how:** A talented chef with a following can't afford a lease. Pop-ups need a host venue, a separate ticketing site and weeks of coordination. Meanwhile, 14 rooms within two miles sit dark on Mondays.

**Why now:** Wonder proved concept and kitchen can be decoupled at scale ([MarketScale](https://www.marketscale.com/industries/food-beverage/wonder-confidential-ipo-filing-grubhub-owner)). Independents are closing and leaving empty rooms ([Business Today](https://www.businesstoday.in/world/story/36-year-low-new-york-restaurant-count-plunges-by-2000-in-q1-2026-amid-soaring-costs-562283-2026-10-18)). Agents can now plan and book multi-step logistics for consumers ([Fox Business](https://www.foxbusiness.com/technology/meta-introduces-muse-personal-ai-agent-can-send-emails-book-travel)).

**Boldest version:** Restaurants become software-defined. A concept, meaning a chef, a menu, a guest list and a booking page, has no fixed location. Each night, a matching engine assigns it to a room based on idle inventory, equipment, party sizes booked and guest travel time. The concept's floor plan adapts to the room (the 2026-11-02 wildcard, Shape-Shifting Room, from the other side), and guests get the address 24 hours ahead. Rooms earn rent from dark nights automatically. Chefs build brands without leases, and a city's restaurant capacity becomes a liquid pool. It evolves the 2026-10-12 wildcard (Dark Hours Exchange), which rented out a venue's closed days. Here the concept is the primary entity and the room is a resource it consumes.

**First wedge:** A booking whose venue is resolved later. A `Reservation` booked against a concept is assigned a `Venue` the day before. The demo uses two seeded venues and the existing floor-plan engine (`apps/hospitality/src/pages/FloorPlanEditorPage.tsx`) to test which room fits tonight's covers.

**What would kill it:** Everything physical: health permits are tied to a premises, liquor licenses to an address, insurance to an operator. Kitchens aren't interchangeable. It is impractical on purpose.

**Charter fit:** It challenges the multi-region scale non-goal in spirit (a city-wide resource pool) and has nothing to do with today's themes. It is here to stretch the model.

**Scores:** ambition 5, feasibility 1, wow 5.

## Pick of the week: Daypart Forge

Daypart Forge has the most leverage. It turns the demo from "reservations for one restaurant" into "the operating system for a room", which is a pitch a struggling owner understands in one sentence: more revenue from the rent you already pay. It builds directly on the newest, most deliberate work in the repo. `VenueService` was designed as "the venue-wide identity of a service", and services already have their own hours, rules and calendar filter. So the wedge is an identity layer, not a new engine. And it showcases the agent platform where it is strongest: an agent drafting a complete concept (name, menu, schedule, page) through the Rialto catalog is a five-minute demo a hiring manager will remember. Standing Table is the safer second bet: smallest wedge, clearest guest value. It also compounds with Daypart Forge (a standing table at the lunch concept).
