## The five

### 1. Agent Hiring Hall

**Pitch:** Hire AI agents the way you hire staff: give each one a job, a trial period and a review, and only promote the ones that earn it.

**Who hurts today, and how:** The owner of two wine bars in Philadelphia has been sold four "AI" products this year: a phone bot, a marketing tool, a profit adviser and an inventory assistant. Each has its own login and its own idea of what it may touch. The phone bot once promised a party of twelve a Saturday the venue had closed. The profit adviser only makes suggestions, so its insights sit unread. The owner can't answer basic questions: what is each agent allowed to do, what did it do last week, was it right, and how do I stop one at 7pm on a Friday? So they either switch them all off or trust them blindly.

**Why now:**

- Agents are multiplying in the back office and at the door (Marble's seed, 2026-11-17: [Unite.AI](https://www.unite.ai/?p=523697)), and incumbents are shipping them as advice-only because autonomy isn't trusted yet ([SpotOn](https://www.spoton.com/blog/spoton-introduces-profit-ai/): "does not make operational changes automatically").
- The payments networks are saying the same thing: human-in-the-loop first, autonomy later ([Visa](https://corporate.visa.com/en/sites/visa-perspectives/innovation/agentic-commerce-expanded-payments-economy.html)).
- Trust in software that acts on a venue's account is at a low: the Toast suit is about charges and add-ons owners say they didn't consent to ([FTC](https://ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/10/ftc-states-take-action-against-toast-inc-over-hidden-unauthorized-fees-deceptive-subscription)).
- Nobody sells the missing layer: a single place where an owner sets, watches and adjusts how much each agent may do.

**Boldest version:** The venue's HR department for software. Every agent, ours or a third party's, gets hired through it with a written job ("answer the phone; book parties up to 6; never waive a deposit"), a permission scope expressed in the venue's own terms (which tables, which nights, how much money), and a probation period where it may only propose. Every proposal and action lands in one ledger. A weekly review scores each agent on real outcomes (proposals accepted, actions reverted, guest complaints, money moved) and recommends promotion, a narrower scope or firing. Agents climb a trust ladder: propose → act with confirmation → act within limits → act and report. Across the network, the anonymised review scores become the industry's only independent rating of hospitality agents, and agent vendors compete to earn "trusted at 400 venues". The owner stops buying AI products and starts staffing a team that happens to include agents.

**First wedge (2–4 weeks):** An agent roster with a trust ladder for the agents the repo already has.

- Each existing tool family (`services/agent/src/routes/gen-agent-{waitlist,closure,availability,guest}-tools.ts`) becomes a named role ("Door agent", "Closures agent") with one setting: `propose`, `confirm` or `act`. The setting lives in venue config (an additive table); it is not an Auth0 role.
- `propose` routes every write through the same gate shape as `apps/hospitality/src/components/venue-onboarding/onboarding-chat-patch.ts`: untrusted tool call → shape check → domain validator → `applied` or `rejected`, shown to a Manager. `confirm` is today's behaviour: the single-use confirmed write in `services/agent/src/routes/gen-tool-support.ts` (`gateWriteTools`).
- A report card per role: proposals made, accepted, rejected, and actions later reverted (the availability undo history, `services/reservations/src/services/availability-changes.ts`), rendered as a Rialto card by a new agent tool.
- A per-role "stop" switch modelled on `services/reservations/src/transitions/venue-policy.ts`, the existing one-place kill switch for outbound effects.
- Promotion is a Manager action only, using the Manager/Host split in `services/reservations/src/services/venue-team.ts`.

It's also the story of this repo itself: the monorepo already promotes its own agents through tier labels, review gates and fast paths. The wedge sells that discipline to a venue.

**What would kill it:** Owners don't want to manage agents; they want outcomes, and a roster can feel like more work. Liability doesn't disappear: when a promoted agent waives a $400 deposit by mistake, the owner still eats it. And if Toast, SevenRooms or OpenTable ship a decent "agent permissions" screen inside their suites, a standalone hiring hall has no third-party agents to govern.

**Charter fit:** Theme (2), operator daily workflow, and the services theme "keep the agent platform boring — proven patterns": this is the repo's own proven pattern. It brushes guardrail 2 (no auth-model changes). It should stay clear of it: agent scopes are venue configuration, never Auth0 roles. Any change to who may promote an agent is HITL.

**Scores:** ambition 5 · feasibility 4 · portfolio wow 5

### 2. Disruption Desk

**Pitch:** When something goes wrong before service, tell the system in one sentence, and it re-plans the whole night — moves, refunds, apologies, a sister venue — for you to approve.

**Who hurts today, and how:** 4:40pm on the second Saturday of December in Minneapolis. The back room's heating fails, and with it 40 of tonight's 110 covers, including a 16-top holiday party with a $600 deposit. The manager has 80 minutes. They work through the book by hand: who to squeeze into the main room, who to call, whose deposit to refund, whether the sister bistro across town can take the party. Three guests aren't reached and arrive to a closed room. The party's organiser posts about it on LinkedIn. The night's real loss is three times the covers.

**Why now:**

- The cost of a disrupted night peaks now: November and December are "often the most lucrative months" and lean on events ([OpenTable](https://www.opentable.com/restaurant-solutions/resources/?p=1332)).
- Forecasts miss by over a quarter even on normal days ([Fourth](https://www.fourth.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/State_of_Restaurant_Operations_2026.pdf)), so plans built around a stable night break often.
- The rest is from first principles: no search turned up a vendor selling whole-book disruption recovery. Incumbents offer bulk-cancel and a message template.

**Boldest version:** Every venue has a calm, rehearsed crisis desk. Any disruption — a broken walk-in, a sick chef, a burst pipe, a blizzard warning, a protest closing the street, a power cut — goes in as a sentence, a photo or a sensor alert. The desk works out what changed (capacity, menu, hours, staffing), runs the impact against every booking, and proposes a complete plan: who stays, who moves to a smaller table or an earlier time, who is offered the sister venue with their history and allergies carried across, whose deposit is refunded and what the apology says in each guest's language. The manager approves in one pass. Afterwards it writes the incident record the insurer wants. Venues run quarterly fire drills against it in rehearsal mode. "We had a disaster and nobody noticed" becomes the normal outcome.

**First wedge (2–4 weeks):** "What happened?" on the Tonight view.

- Free text goes to the agent, which maps it to a closure command the engine already understands (whole venue, a section, shortened hours) using `services/agent/src/routes/gen-agent-closure-tools.ts`.
- The engine does the hard part, unchanged: `closure-impact.ts` groups the affected bookings; `closure-command-plan.ts` checks decisions, finds where a `move` goes and applies the Host emergency limits; `closure-notice-service.ts` and `closure-refund.ts` send notices and refunds; `manage-rebook.ts` gives guests a rebook link.
- New: a "sister venue" move target for groups using `member-venues.ts` (`getMemberVenueIds`), proposed only when the owner runs more than one venue and availability exists there.
- The proposed plan renders as one Rialto review card. Nothing is written until a Manager confirms; the agent's write goes through `gateWriteTools`.

**What would kill it:** A wrong plan at 5pm is worse than no plan: if the agent mis-reads "lost the back room" as "close the venue", guests are cancelled who didn't need to be. Moving guests to a venue they didn't choose can feel like being dumped. And real disruptions are messy (half the room, a reduced menu, two cooks short), while the engine understands closures, not partial capacity.

**Charter fit:** Theme (2), operator daily workflow ("how is tonight going?" at its worst), and services theme (1), reliability of the booking and deposit state machines. No non-goal challenged.

**Scores:** ambition 4 · feasibility 4 · portfolio wow 4

### 3. Day-One Host

**Pitch:** A host on their first shift knows every regular, every allergy and every house rule, because the venue remembers so they don't have to.

**Who hurts today, and how:** A neighbourhood Italian place in Seattle loses its host of four years. She knew that Mr Okafor always gets table 9, that the Friday 7:30 four-top is the landlord, and that the patio heaters need ten minutes. Her replacement starts on a Thursday with a 20-minute walkthrough. On Friday he seats the landlord by the kitchen door, offers a regular the "first visit" welcome, and argues with a guest about a policy that changed last month. He quits in week six. The owner starts again.

**Why now:**

- Turnover averages about 80% a year ([Toast](https://pos.toasttab.com/blog/on-the-line/restaurant-staff-shortage)), and the first 90 days are the riskiest window ([NRA 2026 hiring report](https://go.restaurant.org/rs/078-ZLA-461/images/2026-Research-Insight_Hiring-and-Staffing.pdf?version=0)).
- Managers say FOH needs conflict training and almost nobody gets good training (79% vs 12.5% in [Toast's UK survey](https://pos.toasttab.com/uk/blog/on-the-line/how-ai-is-changing-front-of-house-roles)).
- The repo already holds the venue's memory: guest history, staff notes, occasions, policies.

**Boldest version:** The venue's knowledge stops leaving with its people. Everything the best host knows is captured as they work (notes, seating habits, "always", "never"), and handed back to whoever is on the door in the moment they need it: "Okafor party arriving; he likes table 9; it's free." A new hire's screen starts simple and unlocks as they show competence, the way a game teaches its controls. Before a first shift they rehearse the hard moments (a double-booked table, an angry no-deposit walk-in) against an agent playing the guest. The owner sees each new hire's ramp: shifts until they handle a Saturday without a Manager override. Day one stops being a cost the venue pays every time someone leaves.

**First wedge (2–4 weeks):** First-shift mode for a Host.

- A Host's first N shifts (role from `services/reservations/src/services/venue-team.ts`) see a condensed briefing: the five things tonight that matter, drawn from `briefing.ts` (`BriefingGuest`: visit count, dietary restrictions, staff notes, tags), `guest-recognition.ts` and remembered-on-arrival's occasion flag, on `apps/hospitality/src/pages/BriefingPage.tsx`.
- "Ask the house" in `ChatPage.tsx`: the agent answers policy and venue questions from the venue's own config (cancellation terms via `formatCancellationTerms`, closures, hours), with a citation to the setting it read, never a guess.
- One rehearsal: a scripted conflict scenario on the existing rehearsal timeline (`RehearsalTimelinePage.tsx`).

**What would kill it:** Hosts don't read screens mid-rush; anything longer than a glance is ignored. Most venue knowledge was never written down, so the memory starts empty and only fills if staff write notes, which they don't. And "the system knows the regulars" can tip into creepy if guests learn what's recorded about them.

**Charter fit:** Theme (2), operator daily workflow, and guest-felt: the regular is recognised by whoever is on the door. It sits near the payroll/staffing non-goal but doesn't cross it: no scheduling, no pay, only the work of the shift.

**Scores:** ambition 3 · feasibility 4 · portfolio wow 4

### 4. Mandate Seating

**Pitch:** Let guests' AI assistants book you — safely — because every booking carries a signed note of what the guest actually agreed to.

**Who hurts today, and how:** A tasting-menu restaurant in Brooklyn takes a Saturday booking for six from an AI assistant. The $75-per-head deposit hold goes through on a virtual card. Nobody comes. When the venue charges the no-show fee, the cardholder disputes it: "I never told my assistant to agree to that." The venue has a booking from a bot and no proof of what the human approved. Next time, the owner turns agent bookings off. A guest whose assistant gets refused simply books elsewhere.

**Why now:**

- Card networks have built the missing record: Mastercard's "Verifiable Intent" says who authorised an agent and what it may buy, and it's how Mastercard frames liability ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2026/09/18/mastercard-visa-ai-shopping-payment-land-grab/)). Google's Universal Commerce Protocol has both networks on board ([Digital Commerce 360](https://www.digitalcommerce360.com/2026/04/02/visa-mastercard-in-agentic-commerce/)).
- Platforms' current answer is a ban: Resy deactivated a guest over a booking agent ([Restaurant Business](https://www.restaurantbusinessonline.com/technology/ai-agents-can-help-diners-book-table-it-can-also-get-them-banned)).
- Agent Front Door (2026-10-11) argued for a sanctioned protocol. What's new is that the liability layer under it is now being built by the networks, and a venue can lean on it.

**Boldest version:** Every agent booking is a small, signed contract. The guest's agent presents a mandate: who the human is (verified by their card network or wallet), what they approved (party size, maximum deposit, cancellation terms, dietary needs), and how long it lasts. The venue's engine checks the mandate against its own policy and accepts, counters ("the deposit is $90, not $75; ask your human"), or refuses — in milliseconds, with no phone call. No-shows and late cancels settle against the mandate, so disputes become rare and cheap. Venues set rules by mandate strength ("unverified agents: weekdays only; verified mandates: any night, any party"). Agent bookings become the most reliable bookings in the book instead of the least.

**First wedge (2–4 weeks):** Mandate-backed bookings for one demo agent.

- `services/reservations/src/routes/public-reservations.ts` and `public-holds.ts` accept an optional `mandate` object (party, max deposit, accepted terms text, expiry, signature). Start with the venue's own test agent and a demo signing key; no network integration.
- The engine checks the mandate against the venue's real terms: `formatCancellationTerms` / `evaluateCancellationFee` from `services/reservations/src/services/cancellation-policy.ts`, and the deposit amount from `services/reservations/src/services/deposit.ts`. A mismatch returns a counter-offer, not a booking.
- The reservation stores the mandate hash (additive column). The `forfeited` transition in `deposit-state-machine.ts` records the mandate it settled against.
- The Manager's reservation view shows "Booked by agent, mandate verified" with the terms the human accepted.

**What would kill it:** No standard yet. Verifiable Intent, Visa's tokens and Google's protocol may not converge, and a restaurant engine can't bet on all three. Card networks may keep the mandate inside their own dispute systems, out of the venue's reach. And the volume may stay small for years if Visa is right that full autonomy is a long way off.

**Charter fit:** Theme (1), close the guest-facing loop (agents are now guests' front door), and services theme (1), deposit state-machine reliability. No non-goal challenged.

**Scores:** ambition 4 · feasibility 3 · portfolio wow 4

### 5. Book-to-Buy

**Pitch:** Your reservations already say what tomorrow looks like. Turn them into what to prep and what to order, before you've guessed wrong.

**Who hurts today, and how:** The chef-owner of a 70-seat bistro in Atlanta orders proteins on Sunday night for the week by gut feel. A 30-person rehearsal dinner books Wednesday; she sees it Tuesday and pays retail for extra short ribs. Thursday, half the book cancels for a storm, and $300 of fish goes in the bin. Her reservations system knew both things days earlier. Her ordering spreadsheet didn't.

**Why now:**

- Back-of-house automation is being funded right now (Marble, 2026-11-17: [Unite.AI](https://www.unite.ai/?p=523697)), but its inputs are invoices and inventory — the supply side. The demand side lives in the reservations book.
- 87% of operators saw food-cost increases in H1 2026 ([R365](https://www.restaurant365.com/guides/2026-state-of-the-restaurant-industry-mid-year-report/)); food is a third of every sales dollar ([NRA](https://www.restaurant.org/research-and-media/research/restaurant-economic-insights/analysis-commentary/elevated-costs-continue-to-pressure-restaurant-profitability/)); and food-cost forecasts miss by 14% ([Fourth](https://www.fourth.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/State_of_Restaurant_Operations_2026.pdf)).

**Boldest version:** Reservations become the kitchen's demand signal. Every booking carries a forecast plate mix (from history, party size, occasion and pre-orders); large parties and set menus are pre-ordered at booking; the forward book rolls up into a par sheet and a draft purchase order per supplier, adjusted as bookings arrive and cancel. When a storm empties Thursday, the desk proposes cancelling the fish order before the supplier's cutoff. Cross-venue, a group buys together. The book stops being a seating chart and becomes the restaurant's demand forecast.

**First wedge (2–4 weeks):** Tomorrow's par sheet from the book.

- Covers per service from the forward book (`services/reservations/src/services/availability-snapshot.ts`, `briefing.ts`).
- The owner enters per-cover usage for a handful of key items (an additive column on `MenuItem` in `services/reservations/prisma/schema.prisma`).
- Pre-orders for parties of 8+ on `apps/hospitality/src/pages/ManageReservationPage.tsx`, against the venue's published menu (`routes/public-menus.ts`).
- A par-sheet card on `BriefingPage.tsx` and a `gen-agent-menu-tools.ts` answer to "what do I need for Saturday?".
- No supplier integration in the wedge.

**What would kill it:** Without POS data there is no sales mix, so the forecast rests on the owner's guesses. Walk-ins (a big share for many venues) never appear in the book. And Marble, MarginEdge and the POS vendors own the invoice side; they can bolt on a rough cover forecast faster than a reservations product can learn purchasing.

**Charter fit:** It challenges the "POS integration" non-goal in spirit, because the boldest version wants sales mix. It should move only as a read-only import of item counts, never as a POS. The wedge needs none of it and fits theme (2), operator daily workflow.

**Scores:** ambition 4 · feasibility 2 · portfolio wow 3

## Wildcard: Twin Tables

**Pitch:** Have dinner with someone in another city — same menu, same moment, one table split across two restaurants.

A daughter in London and her parents in Chicago book one reservation across two venues in the same group, or two partnered independents. Both tables get the same menu, adapted to each kitchen. A screen or a projected wall joins the tables at life size. The courses fire in sync: when London's main goes out, Chicago's does too, timed for the six-hour difference (a late lunch for one, dinner for the other). Long-distance birthdays, a wedding toast from the side of the family that couldn't fly, a board dinner across two offices. In ten years the network of "twin rooms" becomes its own category: the restaurant as a place to be together without travelling.

**First wedge (for the curious):** A linked reservation pair across two venues in one `VenueGroup` (`member-venues.ts`), and a shared course "fire" event pushed to both venues' Timeline views over `services/reservations/src/services/sse-connection-manager.ts`. No video in the wedge.

**What would kill it:** Video at a dinner table is awkward. Two kitchens can't hit the same timing. And the market is small and sentimental: lots of delight, little repeat revenue.

**Charter fit:** Guest-felt; challenges the "multi-region scale" non-goal (cross-region venues). It shouldn't move for this; it's a wildcard.

**Scores:** ambition 5 · feasibility 1 · portfolio wow 4

## Pick of the week: Agent Hiring Hall

It's the only idea this week that makes the agent platform the product, rather than a feature inside it. Every hospitality vendor is shipping agents, and the industry's own signals (SpotOn's advice-only design, Visa's human-in-the-loop-first stance, the Toast suit's consent complaints) all point at the same gap: owners don't know what their agents may do, did, or got wrong. This repo already answers that question for itself every day: tier labels, review gates, fast paths, single-use confirmed writes and a validator gate for untrusted agent output (#9437, merged yesterday). The wedge mostly wraps existing tools in a role, a setting and a report card, and it gives every later idea a safe way to ship. Disruption Desk would start as a probationary agent and be promoted once it had earned it. Runner-up: Disruption Desk, which has the strongest code base under it (the closure engine is unusually complete) and the most dramatic five-minute demo.
