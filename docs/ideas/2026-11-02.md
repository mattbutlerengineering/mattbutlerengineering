## The five

### 1. Policy Wind Tunnel

**Pitch:** Before you add a deposit or a second seating, watch a thousand simulated Saturdays play out against it, including the AI assistants that now book for your guests.

**Who hurts today, and how:** The owner of a 70-seat bistro loses three eight-tops to no-shows in a month. She wants a $25-a-head deposit on Friday and Saturday but can't know whether it will cost more bookings than it saves. So she tries it live, gets four weeks of noisy data and a few angry regulars, and reverts. Moving turn times from 90 to 105 minutes, or adding a 5:15 seating, is the same coin flip played with real revenue.

**Why now:**

- Booking demand increasingly arrives through agents that follow a venue's rules literally: ChatGPT through OpenTable, Resy and Yelp since August ([TechJack](https://techjacksolutions.com/ai-brief/chatgpt-now-books-restaurant-reservations-via-opentable-resy/)), and Zest's group concierge since 2026-10-30 ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/30/zests-new-ai-agent-finds-restaurants-and-books-reservations-over-text/)).
- Toast now asks operators to trust "Configurable Agents" acting on rules they cannot test first ([NRN](https://www.nrn.com/restaurant-technology/toast-unveils-an-array-of-new-ai-agents-for-restaurants)).
- Automated red-teaming of agentic systems is an active research area ([arXiv 2609.09647](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.09647)).
- With 77% of operators seeing labor costs rise in H1 ([Restaurant365](https://www.restaurant365.com/guides/2026-state-of-the-restaurant-industry-mid-year-report/)), every booking rule is a margin lever.

**Boldest version:** Every rule a venue has becomes plain-language policy that compiles to config: pacing, turn times, deposits, the cancellation window, holds, waitlist behaviour, and what guest agents may and may not do. No change ships without a run against three sources of demand: the venue's own history, synthetic demand, and an adversarial lane of hostile agents (scalpers, loophole hunters, double-bookers). The output is a forecast band, not a number. Then the platform proposes policy changes itself and shows each one's forecast delta, and the owner approves or rejects it like a code review.

This is the missing test harness that the 2026-10-11 wildcard (the Self-Driving Restaurant) assumed and never specified. It extends 2026-10-12 #4 (Service Flight Simulator) in two ways: from replaying recorded nights to running synthetic and adversarial demand, and from training staff to choosing policy.

**First wedge (2–4 weeks):** A "What if" panel on the availability page.

- The owner picks a draft change: a deposit on or off, a new turn time, or an extra seating.
- The panel runs N seeded simulated nights through the pure engines that already exist: `packages/availability/src/slots.ts`, `resolve-day.ts`, and `packages/cancellation-policy/src/deposit-quote.ts`.
- It replaces the fixed constants in `apps/hospitality/src/components/rehearsal/simulate-service.ts` with a demand profile: booking elasticity to deposits, a no-show rate, and party mix.
- `services/reservations/src/services/availability-preview.ts` already says which real bookings a draft would strand, and the simulation adds the forward-looking half: covers, no-show loss and parties turned away.
- Agent hook: a new tool beside `services/agent/src/routes/gen-agent-availability-tools.ts` answers "what if I add a 5:15 seating?" and renders the result as Rialto catalog UI.

**What would kill it:** False precision. Nobody knows how a single venue's demand responds to a deposit, and confident charts built on made-up priors are worse than no charts. Owners will treat the median as a promise. It survives only if it shows bands, calibrates against the venue's own history, and says "not enough data" out loud.

**Charter fit:** Theme 2 (operator daily workflow), plus the services theme (reliability of the booking and deposit state machines). It is also the strongest agent-platform showcase on this list. It challenges no non-goal.

**Scores:** ambition 4 · feasibility 4 · wow 5

### 2. Second Act

**Pitch:** When you close, sell, or move on, your regulars can come with you, or go to whoever takes the keys, because they said yes, not because a platform owns them.

**Who hurts today, and how:** A chef-owner is closing a nine-year neighbourhood spot because the lease renewal doubled. She has 4,000 guests in her book, but the relationship lives inside her reservation vendor. Her next place opens three months later with an empty list. The new tenant in the old room also starts from zero. The guests who loved the room never hear about either one.

**Why now:**

- Closures are running high: New York's count is reported at a 36-year low with insurance up 7% ([Business Today](https://www.businesstoday.in/world/story/36-year-low-new-york-restaurant-count-plunges-by-2000-in-q1-2026-amid-soaring-costs-562283-2026-10-18)), and Canada is forecast to lose 4,000 restaurants, mostly independents ([Connect CRE](https://www.connectcre.ca/stories/canada-expected-to-lose-4000-restaurants-in-2026/)).
- The platforms that hold those guest lists are consolidating and tightening. Resy is absorbing and retiring Tock ([Expedite](https://www.expedite.news/p/tock-restaurants-are-in-resy-now)), and OpenTable requires primary-system status ([Restaurant Dive](https://www.restaurantdive.com/news/restaurants-respond-opentable-client-agreement-updates-concerns/817594/)).

**Boldest version:** The guest relationship becomes a consented, portable asset with a value. Every venue has a succession plan. When it closes, it runs a consent campaign, and each guest chooses whom to follow: the chef's next project, a sister venue in the group, or the next operator in that room. A verified book (consented contacts, repeat rate, covers history) becomes a line item when a restaurant is sold, and brokers price venues on it. Within five years, a guest's agent can ask a public registry "where did my restaurant go?" and get an answer.

This is distinct from the Switching Engine (2026-10-26 #1, which moves a venue between vendors) and from Guest Passport (2026-10-11 #4, a guest's reliability credential). Second Act moves guests between venues at the end of a venue's life.

**First wedge (2–4 weeks):** A "Last Service" flow built on the closure machinery that already exists.

- An open-ended `DateRule` (`endsOn = NULL`) plus `services/reservations/src/services/closure-notice-service.ts` and `ClosureOutcome` already move or cancel future bookings, notify guests and refund deposits.
- Add a consent question to the notice ("Chef Ana's next place opens in spring. Keep me posted?") using the signed-token pattern from `services/reservations/src/config/unsubscribe-token.ts`.
- Guests who opt in get their `Guest` row copied to a target venue in the same `VenueGroup`, carrying `dietaryRestrictions` and visit history, with a log of what each guest agreed to.

**What would kill it:** Privacy law. Moving personal data when a business is sold or closes is regulated, and consent given to venue A is not consent for venue B. A consent-first design is necessary but not sufficient, so it needs legal review. There is also a market problem: an owner who is closing is exhausted and not shopping for software. That is why the buyer is probably the chef's next venture or the incoming tenant, not the departing venue.

**Charter fit:** Theme 1 (close the guest-facing loop), extended to the end of a venue's life. It stretches the charter's target user to include a venue's whole lifecycle. It challenges no listed non-goal.

**Scores:** ambition 4 · feasibility 4 · wow 3

### 3. The Party

**Pitch:** Book the table once and every guest at it gets their own link: say they're coming, flag the shellfish allergy, pay their share of the deposit. The table resizes itself.

**Who hurts today, and how:** A Friday eight-top is booked under one organiser's name, so the venue knows one phone number. At 6 pm two guests drop out, and the venue holds an eight-top for six. One guest is vegan and never mentioned it. The kitchen learns about an allergy at the table. The per-person deposit is charged to the organiser, who then chases seven Venmo payments.

**Why now:** The group decision itself has moved into agents. Zest's concierge combines everyone's tastes, puts options to a vote and books ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/30/zests-new-ai-agent-finds-restaurants-and-books-reservations-over-text/)). ChatGPT books from inside chat threads ([TechJack](https://techjacksolutions.com/ai-brief/chatgpt-now-books-restaurant-reservations-via-opentable-resy/)). The venue side still receives one name and one number.

**Boldest version:** The party, not the person, becomes the unit the system books. Every invitee is a lightweight guest record with their own RSVP, dietary needs, arrival ETA, share of the deposit, pre-order and share of the bill. Group agents talk to the party object directly, not to the organiser's inbox. Table size, pacing and kitchen prep follow live RSVPs. The organiser stops being an unpaid event planner.

This evolves 2026-10-12 seed #7 (the group dinner planner), which was folded into Event Desk as the low end of event sales. What is new: the scope is every party of four or more, not events, and the other side of the conversation is now consumer group agents.

**First wedge (2–4 weeks):**

- Today each booking has one tokened guest page: `apps/hospitality/src/pages/ManageReservationPage.tsx` with `services/reservations/src/routes/manage-token-scope.ts`. Add per-seat invite tokens.
- Each invitee claims a seat, enters dietary needs (the `Guest.dietaryRestrictions` shape), and optionally pays their share through `routes/public-deposits.ts`, using the `per_person` mode `deposit-quote.ts` already prices.
- Declines shrink the party through `routes/modify-reservation.ts`.
- The host timeline shows "6 of 8 confirmed, 1 nut allergy".

**What would kill it:** Split payments multiply the deposit and refund state machine by N, and that machine is the thing the charter says must be reliable. Invitees may ignore the links. And the consumer apps that own the group thread (Zest and its peers) may never hand the party to the venue.

**Charter fit:** Theme 1 (guest-facing loop: booking, cancellation, confirmation).

**Scores:** ambition 3 · feasibility 4 · wow 4

### 4. Fee Hound

**Pitch:** An agent reads every bill your vendors send (card processor, POS, delivery apps, reservation system, and us), finds the hidden fees and the auto-renew trap, and drafts the way out.

**Who hurts today, and how:** The owner of two cafés is on a "$0 hardware" plan. She can't compute her effective card rate, and she discovers the early-termination fee only when she tries to leave. Three delivery apps send statements in three formats. Nobody reads a processor statement at 1 a.m. after close.

**Why now:** On 2026-10-29 the FTC and eight states sued the largest restaurant POS over exactly this pattern: hidden and unauthorized fees, "$0" plans recovered through transaction fees, and hard-to-cancel contracts with termination fees that are worse for owners carrying a loan balance. Toast disputes all of it ([FTC](https://ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/10/ftc-states-take-action-against-toast-inc-over-hidden-unauthorized-fees-deceptive-subscription), [Restaurant Dive](https://www.restaurantdive.com/news/ftc-states-sue-toast-over-fees-restaurant-dining-online-food/873286/)). With costs still rising ([Restaurant365](https://www.restaurant365.com/guides/2026-state-of-the-restaurant-industry-mid-year-report/)), fee leakage is margin the owner can win back without serving one more cover.

**Boldest version:** The operator gets their own financial counter-agent:

- It ingests every vendor contract and statement.
- It benchmarks effective rates against peers.
- It puts renewal and notice deadlines on the calendar.
- It drafts disputes and cancellation letters.
- When leaving is the answer, it hands off to the Switching Engine (2026-10-26 #1).

The platform also publishes a machine-readable manifest of its own fees and passes its own audit in public. "The software that audits itself in front of you" is the counter-position to the Toast suit.

**First wedge (2–4 weeks):**

- Deposits already run through Stripe (`services/reservations/src/config/stripe.ts`, `routes/deposits.ts`). Step one is a "What we charge" page that itemizes each deposit from Stripe balance transactions: processor fee, platform fee, net to the venue.
- Step two is an agent tool next to `services/agent/src/routes/gen-agent-tools.ts` that takes an uploaded processor statement PDF, extracts the effective rate, flags line items it can't explain, and renders the result as catalog UI.

**What would kill it:** Statements are messy PDFs, and an extraction error becomes a false accusation against a vendor. There is liability in anything that looks like financial advice. A software vendor auditing other software vendors has an obvious conflict of interest. And back-office is crowded (Restaurant365 and others).

**Charter fit:** This challenges the **POS integration** non-goal, at least in spirit. The non-goal exists to avoid maintaining integrations against vendor APIs. Reading a POS's _bill_ touches no vendor API, so the non-goal should be read narrowly rather than moved. Owner economics sits outside the three current hospitality themes, but it squarely serves the charter's test of "a product a small venue would pay for".

**Scores:** ambition 3 · feasibility 3 · wow 3

### 5. Menu for One

**Pitch:** Every guest opens a menu made for them: shellfish dishes gone for the allergic guest, half portions priced for the light eater, and the same price for everyone.

**Who hurts today, and how:**

- A guest on a GLP-1 drug orders off the kids' menu or awkwardly splits an entrée.
- An allergic guest questions the server about every dish.
- The kitchen scrapes half of every plate into the bin.

The menu is one document for very different people.

**Why now:** 87% of GLP-1 users surveyed say they would likely order a smaller version of a regular dish ([Toast](https://pos.toasttab.com/blog/data/glp-1-restaurant-habits)). Chains are already responding: Olive Garden's Lighter Portions went national in January ([Tasting Table](https://www.tastingtable.com/2112138/glp1-menu-trend-2026/)). Toast's Menu Agent shows menus becoming agent-managed ([NRN](https://www.nrn.com/restaurant-technology/toast-unveils-an-array-of-new-ai-agents-for-restaurants)). With 36% of consumers spending less ([NRA Q2 summary](https://www.pulse.bot/food-and-beverage/news/national-restaurant-association-quarterly-consumer-insights-survey-q2-2026-48e2b3fb-3c90-45ef-a8cc-7d349d2584ef/)), a smaller, cheaper plate is how an independent keeps the visit instead of losing it.

**Boldest version:** The menu becomes a generated document per guest, per visit and per course. Portion size is a dial with priced sizes. Dishes recompose around constraints. Tomorrow's prep is forecast from what booked guests' menus emphasise. A guest's agent can read their own menu before arrival. Prices are never personalized: the one-price rule from Menu P&L (2026-10-26 #2) holds.

This is distinct from Safe Plate (2026-10-19 #5, which carries an allergy from booking to plate) and from Menu P&L (costing). Menu for One is the guest-facing composition layer that both of them feed.

**First wedge (2–4 weeks):**

- Add a `MenuItemSize` table (an additive migration, since `MenuItem` has only `priceCents`) for priced half and full portions.
- Add a "Your menu" link on `ManageReservationPage.tsx` that renders `PublicMenuPage.tsx`'s data filtered by the booked guest's `dietaryRestrictions`.
- The agent composes the layout through `services/agent/src/routes/gen-agent-menu-tools.ts` and the Rialto catalog, which turns `apps/gen` from a playground into a guest-facing feature.

**What would kill it:** Without order history (POS is a non-goal), personalization stays shallow: it knows dietary needs, not tastes. Filtering by allergy creates liability if the menu's ingredient data is wrong, so Safe Plate is a prerequisite. Kitchens resist portion variants.

**Charter fit:** Theme 1 (guest-facing loop), and it showcases the apps/gen and Rialto catalog approach on a real surface.

**Scores:** ambition 3 · feasibility 4 · wow 4

## Wildcard: Shape-Shifting Room

**Pitch:** Your dining room rearranges itself every afternoon for tonight's book. Two-tops merge into the ten-top at 7 and split back at 9, because the furniture moves itself.

**Who hurts today, and how:** It's a Saturday with three large parties and twenty two-tops. At 4 pm staff push tables around on a guess. The 7:45 eight-top ends up at four-tops jammed together in an aisle, and good seats sit empty because the room was the wrong shape for the night.

**Why now:** I found no source in the last 30 days on self-moving restaurant furniture, so this is reasoned from first principles. Every earlier entry treats the room as fixed and optimizes the time axis. Sell the Seat (2026-10-12 #3) made seats bookable. The remaining axis is the shape of the room itself.

**Boldest version (10 years):** Layout becomes a scheduling variable. The optimizer solves seats × time × shape for each night. Motorized table bases and partitions reconfigure between turns, and acoustic zones follow the layout. The floor plan editor stops being a drawing tool and becomes a control surface.

**First wedge:** "Tonight's layout." It reads the booked party mix, proposes table merges and splits using `Table` min/max covers and positions in `FloorPlan.layoutJson`, and shows staff a 4 pm move list on the floor plan in `apps/hospitality/src/pages/FloorPlanEditorPage.tsx`. For now, the staff are the robots.

**What would kill it:** Hardware cost, guest safety, fire-code egress rules, and a furniture industry that moves in decades.

**Charter fit:** Theme 2 (operator daily workflow), for the wedge. The hardware is the venue's purchase, not platform infrastructure.

**Scores:** ambition 5 · feasibility 1 (vision) / 3 (wedge) · wow 4

## Pick of the week: Policy Wind Tunnel

Bet on the Policy Wind Tunnel. Every owner fears the same thing: changing a rule and finding out a month later that it was wrong. That fear grows as more bookings come from agents that apply rules literally. The repo is unusually well placed to build it: the availability and deposit engines are already pure functions, rehearsal mode already simulates a night on the real floor plan, and the draft preview already computes what a change would break. The wedge is mostly wiring those together with a demand model.

It also makes the clearest case for the agent platform. The same discipline this repo applies to its own code, where no change merges without a test and the reviewer sees the forecast delta, gets pointed at a restaurant's rules. A venue would pay to stop guessing, and a hiring manager would understand that in five minutes.
